\begin{abstract}
Global social and ecological challenges may be considered as collective action problems requiring sufficient cooperation with pro-mitigation norms, soon enough to be effective. Sociopolitical polarization is a barrier to collective action. Prior agent-based models of behavioral change on structured networks in a shared sociopolitical environment have shown that polarization emerges naturally in such systems and the rate at which polarized clusters can be dissolved limits the rate at which consensus can be reached. Here we study how guided social link rewiring affects the rate of depolarization in both synthetic and empirical social networks. We investigate rewiring algorithms representing random meetings, introduction by mutual acquaintances, and bridging between socially distant communities. We find that building lasting links between polarized individuals and communities can accelerate consensus formation when the sociopolitical environment is favourable. Switching from the homophilic rewiring currently used by social media to heterophilic rewiring can accelerate formation of a cooperative majority by roughly one third. This strengthens the evidence that promoting connection between polarized communities could accelerate collective action on urgent global challenges.
\end{abstract}

\dropcap{A}lthough the urgency of global socio-ecological challenges such as climate action and pandemic containment has become clearer \citep{ipcc_climate_2022, ringsmuth_2022}, governments struggle to enforce policies aimed at tackling them through collective action \citep{ringsmuth_2022,vasconcelos_segregation_2021,pawloff_divergers_2014}. Research shows that political and social polarization around such global challenges is high \citep{carothers_democracies_2019,gladston_social_2019,mccarty_polarization_2019}. Empirical studies have found that high levels of political and social polarization can significantly inhibit collective action  \citep{vasconcelos_segregation_2021,carothers_democracies_2019,axelrod_preventing_2021} and suggest that dissolving polarization could promote efficient policy-making and restore functional democracy \citep{vasconcelos_segregation_2021,pawloff_divergers_2014,axelrod_preventing_2021}.
 In general, a polity is polarized when dominated by two opposite or contradictory tendencies that lack significant common ground \citep{stavrakakis_paradoxes_2018}. This usually also involves increased support for extreme political views relative to more moderate ones \citep{mccarty_polarization_2019}. Since individuals' opinions are highly impacted by their social context \citep{janssen_evolution_2008,gowdy_behavioral_2008,tavoni_survival_2012}, polarization is catalyzed by social structures that reinforce ideological separation by excluding external information \citep{gladston_social_2019,williams_network_2015,nguyen_echo_2020}.

On balance, current evidence suggests that, under some conditions, increasing interaction between disagreeing agents by rewiring their social links might speed up collective action (ref?). However, the precise nature of these conditions and their potential effectiveness are yet to be discerned. Previous modelling work analyzing opinion dynamics has shown that the rate of depolarization is essentially determined by the frequency of interactions between different opinion clusters and the response assumed when opinions are exchanged \citep{axelrod_preventing_2021,fotouhi_conjoining_2018,andersson_dynamics_2021}. To complicate things, there is also evidence that strongly disagreeing individuals will have disagreeable interactions that reinforce their polarization. This `backfire effect' is documented in a number of contexts including online interactions \cite{bail_exposure_2018}, response to media exposure (ref), and in behavioural experiments (ref). It is difficult to estimate the fraction of interactions governed by this effect, which often depends on context \cite{wood_elusive_2019}.

Interaction dynamics in turn depend on who is interacting. Social link rewiring defines the social context. In online social networks, links are rewired by recommender systems that are often designed to maximize business performance metrics coupled with engagement, virality or other advertisement exposure \cite{pappalardo_survey_2024}. This is often incongruent with socially beneficial network states, leading to extreme homophily \cite{stoica_algorithmic_2018,starniniEmergenceMetapopulationsEcho2016}, rich-get-richer phenomena \cite{merton_matthew_1988, su_effect_2016} and other network inequalities \cite{cinus_effect_2022}. Social media in which users are linked asymmetrically (directed networks), with influential users distributing content to large numbers of followers may exacerbate these effects. All of the elements above may lead to increased polarization \cite{santos_link_2021}.


Much prior work has studied interactions between polarization and link rewiring \cite{santos_link_2021, sasahara_social_2021, borges_how_2024, espin-noboa_inequality_2022, ferrara_link_2022, pappalardo_survey_2024}. Specifically, previous studies in network science \cite{ferrara_link_2022, espin-noboa_inequality_2022} thoroughly deal with established recommender algorithms, while other authors \cite{cinus_effect_2022, borges_how_2024, santos_link_2021} deal with opinion dynamics with homophilic/heterophilic rewiring. However, these elements are typically analyzed separately or in subsets. Consolidating the work above by combining empirical topology-based recommender algorithms and more classical opinion dynamics is an important step to 1. bridge understanding between topological changes and cooperation/polarization in opinion dynamics models and 2. provide more empirically grounded testing of these factors in the same models. Both of which have been called for in the literature (ref, ref). Most of all, these points serve here to more thoroughly test the conditions under which cooperation, polarization, and ultimately, collective action arise.

Building on this, we extended an agent-based model (ABM) previously introduced by Andersson et al. \citep{andersson_dynamics_2021} to systematically compare how different link rewiring strategies affect polarization and collective action against a baseline static network, and random rewiring scenarios. The model simulates the spread of a prosocial norm on a given social network embedded in a shared sociopolitical environment.

We studied two widely employed \cite{espin-noboa_inequality_2022} link recommendation algorithms - Who-to-follow (WTF) and Node2Vec (N2V) - alongside our proposed heuristic-based algorithms aimed at improving cooperativity. The first of which is a 'local rewiring' algorithm, the second a 'bridge rewiring' algorithm. Our approach tested these algorithms across four different types of network topology, two synthetic and two empirical (Facebook and Twitter). In addition, we also investigated how the backfirer effect influences the likelihood of cooperation over a larger parameter space to provide a broader perspective on the existing discussion around the phenomena. We demonstrate that homophilic rewiring algorithms can accelerate formation of a cooperative majority by roughly one third compared to homophilic approaches, remain robust to moderate fractions of divergers (up to $33 \% $), and vastly outperform existing recommender algorithms like Who-to-follow, providing theoretical support that promoting connections between polarized communities could accelerate collective action on urgent global challenges.

\section{Results}\label{results}
%\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{subsec1}
To learn how rewiring algorithms affect depolarization and consensus time we study both the transient dynamics and steady state of the `cooperativity' (mean agent opinion, $ \langle a \rangle \ = \sum_{i}^{N}a_i/N$) and polarization (standard deviation of the cooperativity, $P = \sigma(a_1, ..., a_N)$), averaged over an ensemble of 90 simulation runs. Each run simulates a population of $N$ individuals, embedded in a network. As social change depends more on achieving a cooperative majority than winning over the last few holdouts \citep{andersson_dynamics_2021,otto_social_2020,gardiner_perfect_2011}, we examine the convergence rate within the exponential stage of cluster dissolution, as well as steady state cooperation ($\langle a \rangle_{tmax}$). Convergence rates for baseline model parameters and all algorithms are found in Fig. \ref{fig3}. We refer to the discrete-time rate of change in the cooperativity, $\Delta\langle a \rangle / \Delta t$  during the depolarization stage as the convergence rate. The nomenclature $x(y)$ refers to our heuristic algorithms, where $x \in \{local, bridge\}$ denotes the topological constraint, and $y \in \{similar, opposite\}$ denotes the opinion constraint, where similar is homophilic and opposite heterophilic. The results' sensitivity to parameter values are analysed at system convergence, with 30 independent runs per parameter combination (see \ref{fig4, fig5}). We define cooperativity robustness to changes in parameter $\theta$ as $R_{\theta} = \sigma(\{A(\theta_i)\}_{i=1}^n)$, where $A(\theta_i)$ is the cooperativity ratio at parameter value $\theta_i$, computed across $n$ discrete parameter values. The cooperation ratio is the fraction of model runs, $M$ with positive steady state cooperation values, $A = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} \mathbb{I}(\langle a_i \rangle_{t_{max}} > 0)$. This measures the variance of final cooperation (classified either positive or negative) as we manipulate a given parameter. Here, a lower $R$ indicates a more robust response, i.e the steady state is less prone to change. A full account of the model set up, measures, and  parameters is provided in \textit{Methods}. We will proceed by progressively establishing the characteristics of each rewiring algorithm group (recommender and heuristic), and their effects. First against our static and random baselines (sections \ref{recommenders} and \ref{heuristic}), and then against each-other (section \ref{comparing}). Lastly, in section \ref{robustness}, we will report on their performance under a wider parameter space, and show that our findings under our default parameters generally hold.

\subsection{Representative Dynamics}\label{rep_dynamics}

In Figure \ref{fig1}A-F we see that the qualitative stages of the dynamics previously observed by Andersson et al. for a static network \citep{andersson_dynamics_2021} are also maintained in dynamic networks. In the first stage, the slight defector majority at $t=0$ dominates and cooperativity declines. Agents with strong opinions spread them to their neighbours, resulting in growing opinion clusters that gradually polarize the network. Any interactions within the opinion clusters serve to reinforce polarization. Even without assuming bounded confidence \cite{bernardo_bounded_2024}, this cluster formation stage ends in a fully polarized, homophilous state. The depolarization stage can be divided into two substages. In the first, cooperativity grows approximately exponentially. In the second, the convergence rate slows, likely due to the shrinking clusters' finite sizes in combination with fluctuations \citep{andersson_dynamics_2021}. This dynamical stage involves a slower process of cluster dissolution (depolarization), where meaningful interactions take place only at the cluster perimeters, between agents holding opposing opinions. Here the assumption of a slightly positive external field, representing a socio-political environment favorable to cooperation, is pivotal (see \ref{exernalfield}). Although the small positive external field leads to an average high steady-state cooperativity in all scenarios (avg of x), polarization remains high (avg of x). High steady-state polarization is, however, not the case when the proportion of backfiring nodes ($\rho$) approaches $0$ (see section \ref{heatmapsweep}).

\subsection{Recommender algorithms, cooperation and polarisation}\label{recommenders}
 Fig. \ref{fig1} (E and D) shows that the N2V algorithm leads to higher steady-state cooperation than the static reference scenario on the synthetic directed network topology (DPAH). Conversely, WTF diverges substantially from the static baseline. Fig. \ref{fig2} shows that both algorithms fell short of the static benchmark on the Twitter network, and the random benchmark on both directed networks. Polarization, in contrast to cooperation, diverged between the two algorithms. Where N2V leads to the same or slightly higher (x vs x)  polarisation than the static baseline, WTF reduces steady state polarization moderately to x below the static, and y below the random baselines. This was exaggerated on the DPAH  network, where WTF had simultaneously the lowest steady state polarization and cooperation. Both N2V and WTF are dependent on topological structure, but interact with them in different ways.

 The results concerning N2V align with the literature showing that rewiring between structurally similar nodes leads to polarisation \cite{santos_link_2021}. N2V's embedding based algorithms preserve local network structure while gradually increasing clustering. This emergence of clustered, well defined communities prevents opinion exchange except at the boundaries of communities. What this leads to is coexistence of moderate cooperation ($\langle a \rangle \approx 0.5$) and polarization in the steady state.


WTF's mechanism interacts with DPAH network topology to lead to system defection ($\langle a \rangle = -0.5$) through a defective clustering trap.  WTF  reinforces local neighbourhoods by dramatically increasing clustering coefficient (from $\approx 0.3$ to $\approx 0.9$) and modularity (from $\approx 0.2$ to $\approx 0.8$). This structural reinforcement amplifies existing opinion distributions within tightly clustered communities that predominantly contain defective agents from the initial conditions. Then, the circle-of-trust mechanism ensures agents preferentially connect within their existing topological clusters, which converge with defective opinion clusters during early dynamics, creating echo chambers that lock the system into poor cooperativity. This paradoxically reduces polarization ($\approx 0.20$) through homogenization of defective states rather than promoting diverse opinion exchange. A defection trap emerges from WTF's tendency to reinforce whatever opinion distribution exists locally, combined with the initially defector-biased population ($\langle a \rangle_{t_0} = -0.25$). In contrast, on our Twitter network with higher initial modularity and assortativity, WTF exhibits opposite structural behaviour, initially preserving community structure but eventually breaking down modular boundaries (modularity decreases from $\approx 0.7$ to $\approx 0.3$). This allows cooperative pockets to spread across communities, producing moderate cooperation ($a\approx0.25$) and polarization reduction in the steady state. Despite this, it creates severe structural inequality, producing an increase in the in-degree Gini coefficient from $0.5$ to nearly $1.0$. Such a network state concentrates influence among a few highly connected nodes. An overview of network metric evolution for all scenarios is provided in the supplementary.


Our findings here are consistent with previous findings (\cite{ferrara_link_2022}, namely that N2V leads to more desirable outcomes in terms of clustering (moderate increase), and reduced cumulative advantage (Gini coefficients). On the other hand, WTF dramaticlly increased clustering and increased cumulative advantage. The observed discrepancy in convergence speed (x percent) and final cooperation (x percent) for N2V demonstrate that community structure plays a significant role; the DPAH network has an average modularity of $Q=0.20$ vs $Q=0.62$ for Twitter. However, N2V seems to robuster to changes in topology in the context of facilitating cooperation. This contrasts WTF's topology-sensitivity seen in other work \cite{espin-noboa_inequality_2022}.

\subsection{Heuristic algorithms and their variants}\label{heuristic} Immediately visible in Figures \ref{fig1} C and D is that the $x(similar)$ algorithms lead to higher steady state cooperation than the static baseline (x vs y). The converse was true for the $x(opposite)$ algorithms (x vs y). However, the $x(opposite)$ algorithms lead to faster convergence, especially in the early stages of dynamics. We also note that the trajectories of the scenarios with opinion-constrained algorithms (similar and opposite) were more aligned than topologically-constrained algorithms (bridge vs local). Here the average difference in convergence speed was x, in steady-state cooperation y. Figure 2. shows that all of the above also holds when we compare heuristic algorithms against a random rewiring baseline. With a low diverger fraction ($\rho = 0.10$), forcing opinion exchanges between misaligned agents, as in x(opposite), with large opinion differences ($\lvert a_i - a_j \rvert $) and a positive external field ($\phi$) works to quickly produce convergence in the network. Regular interactions between disagreeing agents dominate less frequent backfiring interactions.

The story changes in the second phase of dynamics. We note in Figs. 1 and 2 that the increasing cooperation drops off, and after $t \approx3 \times10^4$, the x(opposite) trajectories enter a slow decline while the x(similar) converge to a steady state. Homophilic variants lead to higher terminal cooperativity by effectively isolating backfiring agents within homophilic clusters. This acts to prevent cascading changes of opinion reversal initiated by divergers in a backfiring opinion exchange with a non-aligned agent. Conversely, heterophilic variants trigger exactly this process, particularly in the second dynamical phase, where exchanges tend to take place at the edge of opinion clusters, between agents of opposite opinions. Importantly, the relative performance of the local and bridge variations depend strongly on the fraction of backfiring agents. In networks without divergers ($\rho = 0$), heterophilic variants x(opposite) achieve faster convergence with the same terminal cooperativity as homophilic variants x(similar). We discuss these critical diverger fractions in \ref{heatmapsweep}. . Notably, the (opposite) algorithms achieve a cooperative steady state ($\langle a \rangle \ > 0.5$) approximately 30\% faster than the (similar) algorithms, but none achieve over 0.x cooperation.


\subsection{Comparing heuristic vs recommender algorithms}\label{comparing}

We've so far looked at heuristic and empirical recommender algorithms separately against a baseline, but when we compare them directly as in Fig \ref{fig2} and \ref{fig3}, we see that on all topologies, our x(similar) algorithms lead to significantly higher cooperation (x vs y), and lower polarisation (x vs y) in the steady state. This trend is starker on DPAH topologies, where the sensitive dependence of WTF and N2V to network structure identified in section \ref{recommenders} does not extend to the $x(similar)$ algorithms (x percent change). When we compare the structural component of our x(similar) heuristic algorithms, i.e local(similar) vs bridge(similar), we do not see the same scale of change. Here we have a difference of x percent in steady state cooperation, in contrast to y percent for N2V and WTF. A smaller difference is observed for polarisation, with an average change of x vs y for DPAH and Twitter respectively.

The convergence rate of WTF is approx x times that of our $x(similar)$ algorithms. Only bridge (opposite) produces similar values (x vs y). There is an apparent trade off between speed to convergence and final cooperation which we see in fig \ref{fig 3}. It's visible that not all trade-offs are equal and that WTF on the DPAH network is furthest to the lower right. That this coincides with it also producing one of the lowest cooperation states, and thus the greatest range of values over the cooperation/convergence space (x to x) further indicates it's high sensitivity to network structure. Ignoring cooperation, WTF still facilitates faster convergence than x \% of other algorithms. This has implications for unfavourable socio-political environments, explored further in section \ref{exernalfield}. The $x(opposite)$ algorithms showed comparably fast convergence on Twitter and DPAH topologies while still maintaining a positive convergence rate.

These comparisons reveal that our heuristic algorithms generally outperform recommender approaches, especially the $x(similar)$ variants on the dimensions of final cooperation and polarization. Additionally, fewer than x\% of algorithms surpassed the static baseline across all scenarios. Patterns of advantage were consistent: $x(similar)$ variants affected higher cooperation, and decreased polarization over the speed of N2V and WTF (x vs y and z). An exception is seen with WTF-driven depolarization on the DPAH network, however this was due to mechanics which also produced defective system states. N2V showed more consistent performance across topologies but was inferior in either convergence speed ($x(bridge)$) or cooperation ($x(similar)$). Relative to random rewiring, only $1/3$ algorithms were quicker to converge on average, and $1/2$ increased cooperativity. WTF and N2V did not form part of these subsets. Furthermore, random rewiring had the advantage of consistent results across topologies. Algorithms on directed networks resulted in slightly lower mean final cooperativity ($\langle a \rangle=0.32$ vs $\langle a \rangle = 0.50$) and convergence rates (see also Fig. \ref{fig3}) compared to undirected networks. This finding aligns with findings which demonstrate in-degree inequalities limit opinion exchange \cite{karimi_homophily_2018}.

\subsection{Backfire effect and stubbornness}\label{heatmapsweep}
We studied how divergers affect the dynamics over a range of plausible diverger fractions ($ \rho \in [0.10-0.8]$). Figure \ref{fig4} shows the results for empirical networks. On average (excluding WTF), Twitter networks led systems to slightly lower cooperation but showed marginally higher robustness to divergers, and stubbornness. The main differences were observed between algorithms, where there were several consistencies across FB and Twitter topologies. diverger fraction values $\rho > 0.25$ were incompatible with cooperative terminal system states ($\langle a \rangle  > 0 $). In terms of final cooperativity, $x(opposite)$ variations were overall more robust to changes in stubbornness, but slightly more to diverger fraction. Local(opposite) and bridge(opposite) variants resulted in cooperative system states with diverger node fractions, $\rho$ $\in [0.0-0.33]$, compared to a range of $[0.0-0.22]$ for lsimilarocal(similar), and bridge(similar). Moreover, both mean cooperation was higher over the parameter space for the (opposite) variants ($ \langle a \rangle \approx -0.30$, vs $ \langle a \rangle \approx -0.40$) , as well as having a larger volume of cooperative majorities, comprising $\approx16\%$ of the total cooperativity landscape for (opposite), and $\approx13\%$ for the x(similar) variants. Interestingly, $x(opposite)$ variants resulted in slightly higher polarization on average, and also a substantially higher fraction of system states showing extreme polarization ($\sigma(a) \geq 0.8$). The fraction of results above this threshold was $f=0.48$ (Facebook), $f=0.54$ (Twitter) for $x(opsimilarposite)$ variants, vs $f\approx0.30$ for both topologies in the $x(local)$ variants. This mirrors recent work which shows heterophilic rewiring more capable of reaching cooperative consensus in polarized social networks \cite{borges_how_2024}.

Although x(opposite) variants were more robust to changes in the diverger fraction, x(similar) variants produced higher mean cooperation: $\langle a \rangle \approx 0.63$ versus $\langle a \rangle = (0.6, 0.56)$ for (FB, Twitter) respectively when we limit results to where the final system state was cooperative. The highest diverger fraction over all networks that still allowed a cooperative system state was $\rho = 0.44$, on the Twitter network with WTF. A counter-intuitive result as systems with WTF demonstrated an equal second lowest average cooperation, and the highest polarization among algorithms. Further, the reference topology for Twitter, DPAH, proved to be more robust in other measures to divergers, with an average diverger fraction of  $\rho = 0.068$ vs $0.064$ (Twitter) , and a lower robustness score, $R= 0.235$ vs $0.257$ (Twitter). This, however, did not translate to a high average cooperation in comparison to other topologies. \section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

Our results show that compared to a static network, a variable network structure can lead to a faster convergence to cooperative states. We saw this generally when links are established to agents holding different opinions e.g in our $x(opposite)$ algorithms, or if they are selected at random.  If instead agents establish links to similar opinion holders when rewiring, or follow the WTF algorithm, convergence is slower than on a static network. In the former, this holds true whether potential neighbours are limited to friends of friends or outside an agent's local cluster, so all $x(similar)$ subvariants. However, convergence speed and steady state cooperativity are decoupled in many scenarios. Non-trivial ($ \rho > 0 $) levels of divergers will lead to differential stages of dynamics,  algorithms with the fastest convergence in early-mid stages of the trajectory may lead to higher polarization, or less cooperativity in the end-stages. Convergence to full cooperation is assisted by a strong pro-cooperation sociopolitical environment, however, cooperation can still emerge in it's absence under certain reconfigurations, particularly those involving heterophilic opinion constraints, i.e $x(similar)$). These findings are consistent with previous findings by Axelrod et al. \citep{axelrod_preventing_2021} and Nguyen \citep{nguyen_echo_2020}, that interactions between polarized clusters can act to maintain polarization when opposite opinions are actively discredited through manipulations of trust. To dissolve such echo chambers, access to agents and information sources outside echo chambers need to be enabled \citep{nguyen_echo_2020, borges_how_2024}. However, we also confirm the efficacy of heterophilic rewiring under increasing diverger fractions ($\rho$) to a limited extent. Cooperation on $x$(opposite) algorithms is shown to be robust to an increase in diverger fraction at middle and higher stubbornness values ($w$), seen in Fig. \ref{fig5}. More so than $x$(similar) algorithms. This novel finding helps support the use-case for algorithms which engage individuals with differing opinions to support cooperativity. It comes with the caveat that individuals must display some resistance to change, or discernment, as opposed to immediate opinion adoption. Some empirical evidence suggests that this is firstly not the case for certain population groups , and secondly not a feature in general of modern digital interaction \cite{bak-coleman_stewardship_2021}. The outcome of simulations under the WTF algorithm showed starkly different results from all others. Firstly, the simulations showed the lowest steady state cooperation under our default parameter configuration. Secondly, amongst the results presented in \ref{fig5}, they were singular in their limited response to an increase in the global field parameter ($\phi$). Although the N2V algorithm is not as performant in terms of convergence speed or final cooperativity with the default parameter configuration compared to other algorithms (except WTF and $x(opposite)$, it showed robustness to divergers, and a non-positive external field. This may be related to the mechanism of N2V recommendations, which rely on embedding-space proximity, not necessarily topological or opinion closeness. Other work has found that this helps mitigate rich get richer network effects \cite{ferrara_link_2022}. Here, this could play a role in disrupting cascading effects which quickly push the system into a fully uncooperative state from the starting conditions ($\langle a \rangle_{t_0} = -0.25$). Which we see otherwise in algorithms which amplify cascading effects, i.e $x(similar)$ and WTF.

**More novel findings**

\subsection*{Limitations\label{limits}}
The simplicity of our social interaction model limits the translation of our findings to the real world. Opinion preference is limited to a binary decision (homo- or heterophily). Digital, and general social interactions assume a continuous distribution of preferences. Similarly, agent stubbornness is fixed and homogenous. Varying the stubbornness between agents would imply coexistence of easily persuaded and intransigent agents. We might expect the former to cluster around the latter, who are effectively `influencers', thus reinforcing polarization \cite{andersson_dynamics_2021}. Furthermore, expanding the current single variable agent opinion state into a multidimensional vector would allow representation of multiple character and cultural traits \citep{Pham2021, Axelrod1997}.  Such a representation could be used to analyse how social media algorithms could be designed to focus on peoples commonalities rather than highlighting their differences \citep{dunbar_anatomy_2018, gladston_social_2019}. Existing studies have shown interaction terms considering multidimensional vectors can substantially affect dynamics \cite{baumannEmergencePolarizedIdeological2021}. Several points above could be treated in a generative ABM approach \cite{vezhnevets_generative_2023} (GABM) using large language models (LLMs) prompted with social media data e.g user profiles. Along with the existing context provided by the LLM training data, this would allow a richer representation of agents e.g with multidimensional traits. More recent work doing this in the context of news sources \cite{tornberg_simulating_2023} shows similar effectiveness of opinion heterophily ("bridging algorithm") in consensus-building with news article recommender algorithms. Similar recent work focusing explicitly on echo chamber formation in online communities found that an equation based social interaction term similar to that used in this paper also perform reasonably compared to an LLM enhanced approach featuring 6 LLMs when validated against real world datasets \cite{gu_large_2025}. Lastly, we assumed that newly established connections are stable whether or not they connect agents holding similar or opposite opinions. This is contrary to evidence that disagreeing individuals tend to avoid contact \citep{dunbar_anatomy_2018}, and that connections are inherently dynamic and face decay \cite{roberts_costs_2011}. Future extensions of this work would address this by adding link decay rates that depend on the agents' states.

\subsection*{Implications\label{futur}}
To reduce time-to-consensus and aid cooperative system states, it is advisable to connect people holding different opinions, even if there is a chance of backfiring social interactions. In comparison to a baseline, these algorithms can reduce the time needed to achieve a cooperative majority by roughly one third. They also demonstrate robustness to moderate fractions of divergers, and less cooperative socio-political environments. Inducing heterophilic connection does not, however, guarantee depolarization or high-steady state cooperativity outside of a relatively agreeable society with a low presence of divergers.

Our results also show that a simple heuristic algorithm featuring homophily as an opinion constraint vastly outperforms a WTF-based rewiring algorithm in enabling social cooperation, and further, so does a random rewiring algorithm. The latter finding supports existent indictments against recommender algorithms conducive to rich-get-richer network-effects \cite{espin-noboa_inequality_2022}. Our results provide theoretical support to the substantial body of evidence that polarization in social media could be limited or reversed if recommender algorithms were modified to promote and maintain links between groups holding opposite opinions (i.e out-groups) \citep{santos_link_2021} (more refs).




\matmethods{
\subsection*{Agent-based model}\label{abm}Each agent’s level of behavioural cooperation is represented by a continuous real-valued variable \( a \in [-1,1] \) and is also referred to as the agent's `opinion' since our formalism borrows from opinion dynamics modelling. However, despite this terminology, we stress that each agent's state variable is intended to represent behaviour rather than just opinion. We refer to agents for whom $-1\leq a<0$ as defectors and $0<a\leq1$ as cooperators. We distinguish between topological clusters, detected using the Louvain algorithm \citep{blondel_fast_2008}, and opinion clusters, which are made up of agents sharing the same opinion. Following Andersson et al \cite{andersson_dynamics_2021}, simulations begin with mildly defecting agents because we are concerned with building collective action from a state of inaction.
\newline

Each independent network simulation iterates eight steps until the network converges to a steady state:

\begin{tabular}{cl}
    (1) & Randomly select an agent \(i\) \\
    (2) & Randomly select a neighbour \(j\) of \(i\) \\
    (3) & Perform interaction between agent \(i\) and \(j\) as described in section \ref{repdyn} \\
    (4) & Update \(i\)’s opinion according to interaction outcome \\
    (5) & Select a non-neighbour \(k\) of \(i\) following the chosen rewiring algorithm \\
    (6) & Possibly establish a new link between \(i\) and \(k\) as described in section \ref{linkrw} \\
    (7) & Randomly select a neighbour \(l\) of \(i\) \\
    (8) & Possibly break a link between \(i\) and \(l\) as described in section \ref{linkrw}
\end{tabular}
\vspace{2mm}

While the probability of being chosen as agent \(i\) is independent of an agent's degree, the likelihood of being selected as a neighbor \(j\) for interaction increases with $j$'s degree. Updating $i$’s behaviour, rather than $j$’s, ensures that agents with more connections are more influential as their opinion states change more slowly over time.



\subsection*{Agent interaction}\label{agentinteraction}
The change in the opinion \(a\) of an agent \(i\) when interacting with a neighbouring agent \(j\) is given by

\begin{equation}
\Delta a_i = \lvert a_i - a_j\rvert [w^- (1-a_i)-w^+ (1+a_i)]\end{equation}

Where \(w^+\) and \(w^-\) are the weights for an agent becoming more or less cooperative respectively. To simplify we set \(w^+ = f(x_{ij},r,\xi\)) and \(w^- = 1- w^+\) and obtain

\begin{equation}\label{eq2}
\Delta a_i = \lvert a_i - a_j \rvert [2f(x_{ij},r,\xi) - 1 - a_i]
\end{equation}

where,

\begin{equation}
    f(x_{ij},r,\xi) =
    \begin{cases}
    0                           & \text{if $x_{ij}+\xi < -r$}\\
    \frac{1}{2r}(x_{ij}+r-\xi)         & \text{if $x_{ij}+\xi \in [-r,r] $}\\
    1                           & \text{if $x_{ij}+\xi > r$}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

and,
\begin{equation}
    x_{ij} = w_i a_i + w_{ij} a_j + \phi
\end{equation}


The function \(f(x_{ij},r,\xi)\) captures the response of the affected agent. The parameters \(w_i\) and \(w_{ij}\) represent the self-weight and the weight between agents \(i\) and \(j\). This captures the local mechanisms of stubbornness as well as the susceptibility, which determines the effect of an agent on another. \(\xi\) is a random noise term that is distributed over the interval \(\pm r\) and represents stochastic external factors that may affect the interaction, such as the mood the agent was in. \(\phi\) captures global effects that influence all agents at all times and can be referred to as the external field. This simulates channels shared by all actors such as relevant political policies, media reporting or the moral hegemony of society\citep{andersson_simple_2020}. In addition to the dynamics described above, we follow Santos et al. \cite{santos_link_2021}, and include a second equation to describe the backfire effect. Where interactions lead to diverging opinions. Due to the difficult in estimating the prominence of the diverger effect in any given social interactions, we set our default diverger fraction quite conservatively, but deal with model sensitivty to this parameter in \ref{heatmapsweep}. Adapting a similar approach to Santos et al. \cite{santos_link_2021}, we define an alternative version of Eq. 2:

\begin{equation}
\Delta a_i = \lvert a_i - a_j \rvert \left[ 2f(x_{ij}, r, \xi) - 1 \right] [ a_i a_j ]^\lambda
\end{equation}
\newline
where \(a_i a_j = -1\) if individuals have opposing viewpoints and \(\lambda \in \{0,1\}\) dictates whether the interaction is governed by a converging dynamic ($\lambda = 0$), or a backfiring dynamic ($\lambda = 1$). In the former we simply obtain Eq. \ref{eq2}. The parameters are chosen according to Andersson et al. \citep{andersson_dynamics_2021} and are summarized in table \ref{tab2}.

\begin{table}[t!]
    \begin{center}
      \caption{Summary of model parameters and variables to provide an overview.}
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.0cm} p{3.5cm} p{1.0cm}}
        \toprule
        Parameter/Variable          & Represents                                                                            & Value \\
        \midrule
        External field (\(\phi\))       & Global factors: \newline Policy regulation, mass media, morals, etc                            & 0.05 \\[0.8cm]
        Self weight (\(w_i\))           & Resistance to influence: \newline Stubbornness, ideological commitment, etc                    & 0.6 \\[0.8cm]

        Initial cooperation    & Initial average cooperativity                                                         & -0.25 \\[0.2cm]

\bigskip
The external field is taken to be \(\phi = 0.05\). It is chosen to be positive in order to reflect a society that incentivizes cooperative behaviour. The agent-to-agent weights \(w_{ij}\) are drawn from a normal distribution with mean \(\mu = 0.5\) and standard deviation \(\sigma = 0.15\). We choose the self-weight to  be \(w_i = 0.6\) as it is assumed that people are more likely to keep their own opinion than change it, but are quite prone to opinion change, nevertheless \citep{mallinson_effects_2018}. The random noise term \(\xi\) is uncorrelated with the interaction and hence assumed to be uniformly rather than normally distributed. The width of the noise is set to \(r = 0.1\). For a more detailed discussion of parameters we refer to \citep{andersson_dynamics_2021}.

\subsection*{Network typology}
Social interactions in our model occur between agents embedded in a network, where they are represented as nodes, and their social contacts as links. We define such a network as a graph $G = (V, E)$ with $V$ the set of nodes $V = \{ v_i, ..., v_n \}$, and $E \subseteq V \times V$ as the set of weighted, directed(undirected) links. All our networks have cardinality  $\vert V \vert = N = 800$. We employ both synthetic, i.e generated networks, as well as empirical networks in exploring the evolution of model dynamics. These categories are split again into undirected and directed examples. For our undirected synthetic network, we focus on clustered scale free networks and use the Holme-Kim clustered scale-free network growth algorithm \citep{holme_growing_2002} with an average degree of \( \langle k \rangle = 8 \). Directly linked agents are referred to as neighbours or friends interchangeably. For our synthetic directed network we use the DPAH (directed preferential attachment with homophily) model, an extension of the BA (Barabsi-Albert) model \citep{espin-noboa_inequality_2022}. Networks based on these models are generated with the NetIn package in python \citep{pynetin}(see SI for parameters). We consider two empirical networks representing two popular digital spaces, Twitter (directed) and Facebook (undirected). \subsection*{Link rewiring}\label{linkrw}

By adding a link rewiring step after interaction, we allow for a variable network structure where the neighbours of agent \(i\) can change over time.  Here we introduce five rewiring algorithms in total. Two of which so called recommender algorithms are taken from literature, representative of real-world rewiring strategies employed by digital network operators such as Google and Twitter \cite{gupta_wtf_2013} and detailed in \ref{reccomendalgs}. The remaining three are heuristic and formulated based on a literature review and introduced in section \ref{heuristicalgs}.  The latter represent limiting cases on extreme ends of probability spectra. Their purpose is to give first qualitative insights into how different ways of restructuring one’s immediate social surroundings can affect the dissolution of polarization. We introduce two algorithm (sub)variants for the heuristic rewiring algorithms, each differing by only one assumption, to isolate the effects of each algorithm. The Who-to-follow algorithm was only tested on directed networks.


\subsubsection*{Recommender Algorithms} \label{reccomendalgs}

\paragraph{Who-to-follow (WTF)} \label{WTFrwmeth}
Introduced by Twitter (now X) \cite{gupta_wtf_2013}, the WTF algorithm seeks to connect a user with similar users. Given a set of users $V$ on a network, it does so by calculating a $ \textit{circle of trust}$ for each user $u \in V$. It then ranks each user $u \neq i $ using the $SALSA$ algorithm \cite{lempelSALSAStochasticApproach2001}. The user with the highest WTF score who is not directly connected to $i$, but connected through $i$'s circle of trust will be recommended to $i$. The $WTF$  score for a given user $u$ can be expressed:

\begin{equation}
\text{WTF}(u) = \text{SALSA}(\text{COT}_u)
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\text{COT}_u = \{v \in V \mid \text{PPR}_u(v) \in \text{top}_k\}
\end{equation}
represents the circle of trust for user $u$, determined by the top $k$ nodes with highest personalized PageRank scores relative to $u$, and $SALSA(COT_u)$ computes authority scores in the bipartite graph formed between nodes in the circle of trust (acting as hubs) and their respective neighbors (acting as authorities).


\paragraph{Node2Vec (N2V)}\label{n2vwmeth}
An algorithmic framework for a flexible, and scalable feature learning for nodes in networks \cite{grover_node2vec_2016}. N2V produces a low-level feature representation of nodes as embedded vectors in a vector space. This representation retains critical information about the nodes which can also be extended to their edges. It does this by optimising the likelihood that a network neighbourhood of a node is preserved based on it's feature representation. N2V lends itself to link prediction tasks on social networks, where it scores exceptionally well in comparison to other state of the art techniques \cite{saxena_nodesim_2022}. These include both heuristic predictors such as the common neighbours, and preferential attachment algorithms, as well as other feature learning algorithms \cite{grover_node2vec_2016}. Each node $i \in V$ is rewired based on the cosine similarity of their respective vector representation in the embedding space. For a given pair of embedded vector projections $(\vec{v}_i, \vec{v}_j)$, the cosine similarity is:

\begin{equation}
    \theta(\vec{v}_i, \vec{v}_j) = \frac{\vec{v}_i \cdot \vec{v}_j}{\|\vec{v}_i\| \|\vec{v}_j\|}
\end{equation}

where $\theta$ is the cosine similarity, $\vec{v}_i \cdot \vec{v}_j$ is the dot product of the vectors, and $\|\vec{v}_i\|$, $\|\vec{v}_j\|$ are the norms of the vectors, respectively. The vector projection $\vec{v}_j$ which maximises \(\theta(\vec{v}_i, \vec{v}_j)\) with respect to a given \(\vec{v}_i\) will be recommended to $i$. More information on N2V and a reference implementation can be found on github \cite{groverAdityagroverNode2vec2025a}. For our model, we use a SNAP implementation in C++ \cite{snapSnapstanfordSnap2025}.

\subsubsection*{Heuristic Algorithms} \label{reccomendalgs}

\paragraph{Random rewiring}\label{randomrwmeth}
Random rewiring is taken as a baseline scenario in order to evaluate the role of unguided network change. Agent \(i\) establishes a link to a random non-neighbour \(k\) with a probability \(p\). With a probability \(q\) a random agent \(l\) from the pool of neighbours of agent \(i\) is chosen and the link connecting the two is cut. \(q\) does not change with the number of neighbours. The finding that cognitive capacity and available time limit the number of friends an individual is able to hold \citep{dunbar_anatomy_2018} is included only indirectly, as an agent with high degree has a higher likelihood of being chosen as the neighbour with whom a link is cut. To maintain a stable average degree we assume \(p=q\).


\paragraph{Local rewiring}\label{localrwmeth}
The local rewiring algorithm includes two tendencies found in empirical studies to simulate a more realistic, yet simplified way of establishing friendships. Firstly, an individual's social surroundings influence the pool of people they are likely to meet, making them more likely to engage with people who are topologically close \citep{santos_link_2021,nasiri_new_2021,dunbar_anatomy_2018}. Secondly, individuals' engagements are subject to homophily, meaning they will most likely show preferential attachment to individuals with whom they have a lot in common \citep{williams_network_2015,dunbar_anatomy_2018,kozma_consensus_2008}.
\newline
For implementation we use an approach similar to Crawford et al. \citep{crawford_resisting_2018}. Figure \ref{fig6}A illustrates our assumptions. The color of a node depicts its opinion. We focus on agent \(i\) and limit the pool of available agents \(k\) for rewiring to those non-neighbours that are within \(d\) network steps. These are marked with dashed circles. We set \(d = 2\) (i.e. agent \(i\) can only choose among friends of friends). This is a practical choice as \(d = 3\) (\( \langle k \rangle ^3 = 512 \)) would allow agent \(i\) to establish links with almost half of our chosen network. For breaking a link, a random neighbour \(l\) of agent \(i\) is selected. To maintain a constant average degree, a link is broken if and only if a link is established.

\paragraph{Bridge rewiring}\label{bridgerwmeth}
In the bridge rewiring algorithm we incorporate recommendations for dissolving polarization. These mainly focus on the role of inter-community interactions and highlight their importance as information channels to increase individuals' exposure to differing views \citep{andersson_dynamics_2021,gladston_social_2019,williams_network_2015,lee_homophily_2019,fotouhi_conjoining_2018}. We implement bridge rewiring as a targeted mechanism that deliberately aims to increase connectivity between disparate communities. As figure \ref{fig6}C illustrates, we limit the potential new neighbours of agent \(i\) to the non-neighbours outside of \(i\)'s own cluster. Clusters are identified by the Louvain algorithm \citep{blondel_fast_2008}. Nevertheless it is likely that multiple communities of defectors separated by ones of cooperators exist within a network.

\paragraph{Rewiring variations}\label{rwvar}
 Two additional assumptions are tested in the local and bridge rewiring algorithms. The topological constraint places a limitation on the pool of potential neighbours. We then add an opinion preference. A notation of (similar) and (opposite) is added to indicate whether links are established only if agents are of the same opinion (i.e homophily) or only if they are of a different opinion (i.e heterophily). Local(similar) therefore refers to the rewiring algorithm in which agent \(i\) chooses a random agent \(k\) within two network steps and establishes a link if they are of the same general opinion (see figure \ref{fig6}) i.e if  \(a_i \wedge a_k < 0\) or \(a_i \wedge a_k \geq 0\). Local(opposite) describes the inverse, i.e if \(a_i \geq 0 \wedge a_k < 0\) or \(a_i < 0 \wedge a_k \geq 0\).
 Bridge(similar) (see  figure \ref{fig6}D) and bridge(opposite) (see  figure \ref{fig6}C) both limit the potential neighbours of agent \(i\) to non-neighbours outside of \(i\)'s own cluster. However, a link is established only if the agents agree in the former, and only if they disagree in the latter.
}
